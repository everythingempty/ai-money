An argument · 10 min · November 2026

AI money.

Retail is the last money in the market still moved by fingers. Here's why that ends, why it won't make anyone richer, how early it is, and what has to get built first. Starring Ken, 36, who has a mental note.

01

Meet Ken. Ken has a mental note.

Ken is thirty-six. Mid-level at a logistics company, two kids, a mortgage that knows his name. He has about thirty grand that isn't the emergency fund and isn't the kids' money. "Play money," he calls it, with the confidence of a man who has never once watched it play. Some ETFs, four stocks he has opinions about, a bag of ETH and SOL, and a wallet with USDC in it from the last time he took profit and felt like a genius for eleven minutes.

Here is how Ken's money moves today.

Fingers money
7:40am

On the train. NVDA reports after the close. Ken has a view, and it's a good one: if they guide down, he's out of his calls and into the semis ETF. He makes a mental note. The mental note is very confident.

10:00am

Meetings until 6:30. The mental note is dead by 10:15. Nobody attends the funeral.

7:15pm

NVDA guides down. Ken is holding a toddler and a towel. By morning the calls are down 40%. He sells them at lunch the next day, at the low, in the three minutes between two calls, wearing the face of a man who was right about everything except the part where you have to actually do it.

All year

The USDC from March is still in the wallet earning nothing, because moving it to the vault his friend mentioned means bridging and checking the TVL, and he keeps meaning to do it on the weekend. That was thirty weekends ago.

Saturday

A friend posts a trade in the group chat and it's working. Ken wants it at half size. By the time he has the app open, the pair found, the size typed and the slippage fixed, the move is over and the friend is posting the screenshot.

One thing in Ken's financial life works flawlessly: $200 a week into an index ETF, automatically. It's the best-behaved part of his portfolio, and it's the only part his fingers never touch. Ken hasn't noticed this. Ken is busy.

Now rewind the Tuesday.

AI money
7:40am

The train. Ken types one message: "If NVDA guides down tonight, dump my calls at the open and put half into SMH. If they guide in line, do nothing." His butler reads it back, and it's pickier than Ken was: "guides down" means next-quarter revenue below the midpoint of what the street expects; here's the most it will spend; here's the off switch. One press. Train arrives.

7:15pm

The butler reads the release, decides that is indeed what just happened, queues the exit for the open, sells, buys SMH, and sends a two-line receipt. Ken is bathing the kids. Was it a good trade? Ask again in a month. Most of Ken's trades aren't, and this one has the same odds as always. The difference is that it happened: the thing Ken said is the thing that occurred, at the price he meant, not the price his lunch break could find.

Months ago

He'd also said, once: "Keep $500 of USDC liquid, park the rest in the best stablecoin yield above $100M TVL, and move it if something better shows up." It bridged. It deposited. It tells him once a month what it earned, in a sentence, not a dashboard.

Saturday

Same group chat. "@butler do what he did, half size, $300 max." Done before the next message lands. Ken posts the screenshot this time. If it goes wrong, it goes wrong by $300, which is the number Ken picked, not the number Saturday picked.

That's AI money. Not a robot picking stocks for him. Not a strategy builder with forty toggles. And not, let's be clear, a way to beat the market: Ken's ideas are still Ken's ideas, and executing an average idea perfectly gets you an average result, on time. What it is: his own ideas, said once, in English, then actually carried out, at the right moment, across whatever venue or chain it takes, inside a limit he set, while he lives his actual life.

Fingers money moves when you're looking at it. AI money moves when it should.

Three things happened in that story that no stop order, grid bot or robo-advisor has ever done. It understood a condition written in words ("guides down"). It did several steps in a row, across venues and chains, each gated on the last. And it stayed inside a budget without asking permission every time. If you need the labels: systematic, multi-step, conditional, supervised. Ken doesn't need the labels. Ken needs the Tuesday.

02

Why this is inevitable, not just possible

Three things changed in eighteen months. Two things never change.

The sentence is the program.

Everything Ken wanted is a small program. "If this, then that." "Watch this, move that." For twenty years the only retail traders who had programs were the ones who could write MQL5 at midnight or configure a grid bot without crying, and that was never more than a few percent of anyone. Now the model writes the strategy, the checker and the schedule. The cost of turning an intention into something that executes went from "hire a developer" to "finish your sentence."

Every venue built a door this year.

Between November 2025 and September 2026 a door for agents opened at Alpaca, eToro, Public, Robinhood, Coinbase, Webull, Binance, OKX, Bitget, cTrader for the FX crowd and Phantom for the wallet crowd. A year ago there were a couple of developer-grade hatches. Now there's a door at every place Ken keeps money, and the venues are competing on whose agent gets through it first.

Humans want to feel something, and they're lazy about it.

Nobody active-trades to get rich. People say they do; the data has disagreed for twenty-five years. In the classic study, the most active fifth of individual investors earned 11.4% a year while the market returned 17.9%, and the investors who said they enjoyed investing traded twice as much as everyone else. People trade because it feels like something, and in 2026 that urge is winning everywhere you look: prediction markets went from two billion dollars a month to seventy-five in a year, and same-day options are a third of all options volume. That urge decides whether Ken plays. Laziness decides how much effort he'll put up with per unit of feeling, and that number has fallen with every interface. Nobody stopped phoning their broker because the website was more powerful; they stopped because it was less effort. Then the app beat the website. Now a sentence beats the app. Robo-advisors stalled below 1% of the world's wealth because they made you fill in a questionnaire and then gave you nothing to feel. Chat asks you nothing you weren't already about to say, and does the one thing people actually want, which is the doing.

And the two constants: playing loses, and people play anyway.

Fingers panic, forget, chase and get bored. Retail's share of US stock trading went from 35% in April 2025 to 9% in October 2026, not because people got poorer but because they got scared and found a football parlay. In India, where the regulator actually counts, 88% of individual derivatives traders lost money in the year to March, about ten billion dollars between them, while 99% of the profit on the other side was made by firms running code. Here is the part the pitch decks leave out: giving Ken an agent does not move him to the other side of that table. The other side is market makers and prop desks earning the spread off everyone who takes it, and an agent makes Ken a faster, more faithful taker. Retail FX already ran this experiment, with free bots, fifteen years, and the most automated retail segment on earth, and 74 to 89% of accounts still lose. So AI money is not inevitable because it fixes the losing. It's inevitable because people were going to play anyway, the sentence is now the cheapest way to play, and every venue just opened a door for it. Institutions crossed from fingers to systems between 2003 and 2009 and never went back. Retail will cross for a different reason and arrive at a different place.

03

How early we are. One chart.

Half a percent of Robinhood's customers have opened an agentic account, and they keep about a thousand dollars in it. That's the entire new category, measured honestly.

That's not the weak spot. That's the pitch. In 1997, 17% of US retail trades happened online. In 1998 Merrill Lynch's brokerage chief called do-it-yourself online investing "a serious threat to Americans' financial health." In 1999 it was 48% of retail trades, and Merrill launched online trading. Once the interface exists, the crossing takes a few years, not a few decades. The distance between 1% and 80% is the market. To be precise about what kind: it's the distance between pressing and not pressing, not the distance between losing and winning. Nothing in this chart says retail will trade better. It says whose hand is on the button.

04

What has to get built. In this order.

The venues built the doors. Everything between Ken's sentence and a safe execution is still missing.

  1. Trust primitives: set sober, enforced drunk.

    A limit the agent physically cannot cross, enforced under the model, not in the prompt: which assets, how much per order, how much per day, how much you're allowed to lose this month, when it expires, a kill switch. You confirm the mandate once, with a press, and anything that leaves the account; inside the mandate, the agent runs. Two rules make it real. Lowering a limit is instant; raising one takes until tomorrow, so Saturday-night Ken cannot overrule Sunday-afternoon Ken. And approve-every-trade is the wrong default, not the safe one: people forgive what they don't watch and abandon a machine the first time they see it err, so a tight limit with receipts after the fact earns more trust than a veto on every order.

    One more thing nobody building this wants to hear: somebody has to stand behind the outcome. "We do not supervise, monitor or audit the agent, you carry the risk" is asking Ken to delegate while refusing the one thing that makes humans delegate. Ken gives an agent $1,000 until he trusts it and $10,000 after. Whoever earns that second step wins everything downstream of it.

  2. Deterministic execution.

    Let the model plan. Let something boring execute: orders that can't double-fire, slippage caps, retries, an unwind when step three of four fails. A clever plan executed approximately loses to a dumb plan executed exactly. None of this makes Ken win. It makes sure the thing that happened is the thing he said, which is more than his fingers ever managed.

  3. Conditional instructions.

    "If NVDA guides down" is a standing order, not a trade. Something has to be awake at 7:15pm, watching prices, the portfolio, the chain and the news, cheaply enough that a $200 trade can afford the watching. Today nearly every agent on earth acts only when a human opens the chat. That's a very expensive intern who only works while you're staring at him.

    This is also where the sentence actually beats the button. On the hot trade, "buy this now, I'm feeling it," a big number and a button will always be faster than typing, and Kalshi and Axiom already own that. The sentence wins the cold half: the stop he never sets, the "sell half if it doubles," the Friday rotation, the Sunday limit. The boring half of trading is the half nobody does, and it's the half an agent is for.

  4. Coding capability.

    The agent that can write and run its own code, a backtest, a monitor, a bespoke trigger, serves any idea instead of a menu. This is the why-now turned into a feature. The butler codes so Ken never has to. One warning: a backtest anyone can generate is a backtest nobody should trust, so the agent that writes it should be the one that says so.

  5. Economics.

    A $1,000 account cannot pay for frontier inference on every decision. The cut of the flow has to pay for the thinking, and the thinking has to get cheap: small models for the watching, big ones for the deciding. And be honest about what "a cut of the flow" means. Revenue goes up when Ken trades more, and Ken trading more is, on average, Ken losing more. That is the economics of a casino. Casinos work. The ones that keep working are the ones that enforce the floor against their own top line, because the alternative is that someone else enforces it for them.

  6. Reputation.

    Nobody delegates to a stranger. Agents and mandates need track records people can see. Copy-trading made strangers trustworthy with a leaderboard; this needs the same, so that "approve every trade" becomes "approve the exceptions" because of evidence, not because a default flipped. The leaderboard is also how the Group Chat Guy trusts his friend, which is how the thing spreads.

  7. Onchain for the rails. Regulated for the floor.

    The first draft of this essay said: skip the regulators, wait for the money to come onchain, the rules will be code. Half of that survives. Onchain rails are genuinely better for the crypto half of Ken's money: the spending limit is a smart-account policy rather than a terms-of-service paragraph, a multi-step trade on one chain is one transaction, every action is a public receipt, and the agent's identity is a wallet. Build there.

    The other half doesn't survive, and the dopamine is why. The one retail boom of 2026, prediction markets, happened on CFTC-regulated venues: Kalshi and Polymarket US took more than 90% of October's volume while Polymarket's offshore onchain exchange, the one that got fined and geoblocked in 2022, shrank to single digits. The regulated version of the degen product won. And look at what regulators actually ban. Never the demand; that has never worked once. They ban accelerators: the UK cut betting-terminal stakes from £100 to £2 and outlawed autoplay on slots, ESMA capped CFD leverage, India's derivatives rules cut individual participation by a fifth in a year, Massachusetts went after confetti. "An agent that executes any idea, 24/7, while you sleep" is an accelerator by construction. It is autoplay with a vocabulary. So the move is not to hide from the rulebook onchain. It's to show up with the floor already built, limits, cooling-off, loss caps, before someone writes it for you, and writes it worse.

  8. The one that matters most: a product a lazy person will actually use.

    Every item above can be shipped as a dashboard with forty settings, and it will be used by the same 2% who ran bots in 2015. The point is the other 98%. One app. One chat. Say it once. The agent asks its two follow-up questions, shows you what it's about to do, and does it. No strategy builder. No API keys. No "bring your own model." If Ken has to configure it, Ken will not do it. Ken will keep the recurring buy and nothing else, exactly like today.

05

Two kinds of trader. One butler.

"People don't want AI making their money decisions." Gartner says 11% of American consumers would let an AI decide a purchase. But people delegate judgment all day: to the fund manager, to the map app, to the pilot. What they won't delegate is the part they enjoy, and what they won't forgive is a machine they watched make a mistake. Two kinds of people trade on the side. Neither wants to hand over the idea. Both want to hand over everything else.

The Spreadsheet Guy.

Reads the filings. Keeps a model. Knows exactly what he's up against, which is desks with code, data and no need for sleep. He will not beat them. Nobody with a Python script and a day job beats them, and an agent doesn't change that. What the agent changes is that his ideas get executed the way he wrote them, every time, without the version of him that panics at lunch, forgets the stop, or is asleep. He's not buying alpha. He's buying fidelity. He also wants the forty settings, and he can have them, behind a door the next guy never sees.

The Group Chat Guy.

Wants to buy whatever the meme is, copy a friend at half size, bet the earnings, feel something on a Saturday. Has been told to be more disciplined and does not care. He doesn't need a quant team. He needs a butler. "Do what he did, half size." "Sell half if it doubles." "Buy $50 of whatever Elon tweets about in the next hour." The idea stays his. The feeling stays his. The butler eats the twelve taps, the wrong chain, the fat finger and the stop he forgot to set. For the hot trade he'll still smash the button, because it's faster. The butler gets the rest of his week, and the Sunday limit that Saturday can't raise.

The pro wants limits so the agent can't wreck his plan. The degen needs limits so Saturday can't wreck his month. The pro wants a track record to trust the agent. The degen wants a leaderboard to trust his friend. One butler. Two moods.

The rest of the objections, quickly.

"Bots have existed for twenty years."

They required you to code them, and they weren't fun. Both fixed.

"Agentic accounts are toy money."

So were online brokerage accounts in 1997. Watch the median balance, not the headline.

"If 88% lose with fingers, won't they lose faster with agents?"

Left alone, yes, and spectacularly: an agent with no limits is autoplay on a slot machine. The honest promise is smaller than "you'll win." You'll lose what Sunday-you decided you could afford, not what Saturday-you decided in the moment, and the trades that happen will be the ones you actually meant.

"So it's a casino with a chatbot."

Active retail trading has been entertainment with a loss rate since the first ticker tape; the chatbot didn't do that. What the chatbot can do, and the button never did, is hold the floor: limits set sober, enforced drunk, by the house, against its own revenue. That's the whole difference between a casino and a casino people are allowed to keep.

"Why not just go onchain and skip the regulators?"

Because the one retail boom of 2026 happened on regulated venues, and because what regulators ban is accelerators, and this is one. Build the floor first and bring it with you.

AI money isn't a new asset class or a new strategy, and it won't make Ken rich; nothing about Ken's ideas changed. It's the end of a job Ken never applied for: unpaid, part-time, emotional execution desk for his own savings. He keeps the ideas. He keeps the feeling. He sets the floor. The fingers retire.
